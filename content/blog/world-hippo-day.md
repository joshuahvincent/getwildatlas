**Happy World Hippo Day!** Every February 15, people all over the world celebrate the hippo — one of the most surprising animals we know.

*Read this one out loud together — it's short, and it's more fun with a hippo-grunt voice.*

Hippos make their own sunscreen — and it comes out red!

<figure class="story-figure">
  <img src="/assets/blog/world-hippo-day/hippo-cover.jpg" alt="A hippo striding through shallow water">
</figure>

It's not blood. It's not sweat, either — hippos don't really sweat the way people do. Their skin makes a clear fluid that turns red in just a few minutes. It works a bit like sunscreen, and it may even help fight germs.

A hippo looks like an enormous pig — but apart from its little cousin the pygmy hippo, its closest cousins are whales and dolphins!

Here's another surprise: hippos can't really swim. Their bones are too heavy to float, so a hippo walks and bounces along the bottom of the river instead.

Can you hold your breath like a hippo? A grown-up hippo can hold its breath for up to five minutes before coming up for air.

<figure class="story-figure">
  <img src="/assets/blog/world-hippo-day/hippo-scale.jpg" alt="A hippo standing next to a 5-year-old for scale">
</figure>

A big hippo stands taller than you — head and shoulders taller. A big male hippo can weigh more than 100 five-year-olds — all standing on one big scale together!

<figure class="story-figure">
  <img src="/assets/blog/world-hippo-day/hippo-weight.jpg" alt="A scale showing a hippo's weight compared to more than 100 five-year-olds">
</figure>

Can you picture 100 friends on one big scale?

A baby hippo, called a calf, weighs about as much as two five-year-olds when it's born. Calves can even drink their mom's milk underwater — their ears fold shut and their nose closes tight so no water gets in.

<figure class="story-figure">
  <img src="/assets/blog/world-hippo-day/hippo-calf.jpg" alt="A baby hippo calf">
</figure>

Hippos live together in family groups, usually 10 to 15 hippos. Their eyes, ears, and nose all sit on top of their head, so they can watch the world while most of their body stays hidden underwater.

At night, hippos climb out of the river and munch grass — about 40 kilograms (88 pounds) of it. That's a lot of grass!

### For grown-ups

Hippos are listed as Vulnerable, mostly from habitat loss, hunting, and conflict with people along shared rivers. There's a hopeful story: hippo numbers in Virunga National Park fell from about 29,000 in 1974 to fewer than 1,000 by 2005. The park credits ranger patrols and safer access for the comeback, with drones helping to count the hippos — a February 2026 census found 171 groups and 153 calves, and the park now estimates more than 2,700 hippos, even after a hard 2025 anthrax outbreak. The recovery kept going.

Hippos need clean rivers with room to live. This week, try turning off the tap while brushing teeth, or picking up litter near a pond. It won't reach Virunga's rivers directly, but it's the same idea: rivers stay healthy when the people near them look after them.

Starters: "What do you think a hippo's whole day looks like?" "How can people and hippos share a river?"

If your kid asks *would a hippo hurt me?* — this holds up: "Hippos aren't looking for trouble, but they're big and fast, and they really want their space, kind of like you might not want a stranger walking right up to you. That's why people who study them always stay far away and let hippos be hippos."
