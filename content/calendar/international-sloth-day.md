  grownups: |
    Brown-throated three-toed sloths — the kind in this post — are listed as Least Concern, but they're losing forest in many places. Deforestation pushes sloths down onto roads and power lines they never evolved to cross, and some tourist stops let visitors pay to hold a wild sloth, which stresses the animal. (The pygmy three-toed sloth, a smaller cousin found on one tiny island in Panama, is in much more serious trouble — but that's a different species from our brown-throated three-toed sloth today.)

    The hopeful part: the Sloth Conservation Foundation has been building rope "Sloth Crossing" bridges through the rainforest canopy in Costa Rica, so sloths and other animals can cross roads and gaps without ever climbing down. The foundation says it had installed 374 bridges by the end of 2025, reconnecting more than 8,000 hectares of forest, and it installed its 400th bridge in July 2026.

    This week's hope action: wild sloths should be watched, not held. If you ever see one in the wild, stay back, stay quiet, and let it hang out — and if you see a photo of someone hugging a wild sloth, tell a grown-up that sloths are happiest left in their trees.

A sloth's fur is like a tiny garden — green algae grows right in it, and little moths live there too!

{% figure "/assets/blog/international-sloth-day/cover.jpg", "Three-toed sloths climb slowly through the rainforest canopy — tropical rainforest canopy of Central and South America" %}

Meet the three-toed sloth — one of the slowest-moving mammals in the world. It hangs from branches using three long, curved claws on every hand and foot, like built-in hooks, and it almost never lets go.

Can you hang your arms over a couch cushion the way a sloth hangs from a branch?

{% figure "/assets/blog/international-sloth-day/scale.jpg", "An illustrated three-toed sloth standing next to the Wild Atlas explorer kid, with a measuring bar showing 60 centimeters" %}

A grown-up sloth is about as long as your arm and shoulder together — around 60 centimeters. Line up about two sloths nose to tail, and they'd be as tall as you!

{% figure "/assets/blog/international-sloth-day/weight.jpg", "Four illustrated sloths on a scale next to the Wild Atlas explorer kid on a matching scale" %}

It's light, too — it would take about four sloths to weigh as much as you. A sloth moves so little that in a whole day, it might only travel about as far as across a playground. But don't count it out: sloths are surprisingly good swimmers.

Here's a wow fact: most animals with fur, like us, have seven bones in their necks — but a sloth can have up to nine! Those extra bones let it turn its head almost all the way around.

Can you turn your head almost all the way around, like a sloth's extra neck bones let it do?

About once a week, a sloth climbs all the way down its tree — just to poop. The moths that live in its fur ride along and lay their eggs in the poop. When the baby moths grow up, they fly up into the trees to find a sloth of their own.

{% figure "/assets/blog/international-sloth-day/baby.jpg", "A baby Brown-throated Three-toed Sloth in the rainforest canopy, clinging to its mother" %}

A baby sloth is born weighing less than a big bottle of water. It hangs onto its mom's tummy and rides along for months, learning which leaves are good to eat.

{% figure "/assets/blog/international-sloth-day/habitat.jpg", "A three-toed sloth swimming slowly across a calm brown rainforest river, head above the water" %}

Can you move as slowly as you possibly can across the room, like a sloth?
