    The giant Pacific octopus is Least Concern — not considered at risk. But nobody actually knows how many there are in the wild, so scientists keep watching. Divers who loved these octopuses spoke up after one was legally taken from a favorite Puget Sound dive site — 5,000+ signatures in five days — and the state voted to protect giant Pacific octopuses at seven dive sites there.

    Try tonight: "Did you know an octopus has three hearts?" "What do you think it would be like to taste with your arms?" "If you had eight arms, what would you do with them?" "Why do you think an octopus changes color?" "What do you think the ocean needs to stay a good home for octopuses?"

    **"What happens to the mommy octopus?"** A mother octopus lays tens of thousands of eggs and then guards them for about six months without ever leaving — she doesn't even go eat — until they hatch. Once her babies swim off into the ocean, her job is done, and she dies soon after. It's sad, but it's also how new octopuses get such a good start: their mom spent everything she had keeping them safe.

    **One hope action:** at the beach or a tide pool, look and don't take. If you turn over a rock, put it back the way you found it. That's someone's home.

{% figure "/assets/blog/world-octopus-day-2026/cover.jpg", "A reddish-brown giant Pacific octopus resting on a cold, rocky reef, its arms curled over the rocks", "The giant Pacific octopus — the biggest octopus we know of." %}

Meet the giant Pacific octopus. It lives in the cold, rocky part of the ocean — the North Pacific, all the way from Japan to Alaska to California.

It has **three hearts.** Two little ones, and one big one. Can you count to three hearts on your fingers?

Its blood is **blue**, not red like ours. That's because it carries oxygen with copper instead of iron, and that copper turns blue when it's carrying oxygen.

Here's the wildest part: it has one big brain, plus a mini-brain in every single arm. That's nine "brains" altogether — one big one in charge, and eight helpers, one per arm, that let each arm feel and move on its own. Can you wiggle eight fingers and pretend each one can feel things all by itself?

Every arm is covered in suckers, and every sucker can **taste.** It tastes rocks, shells and crabs just by touching them. Can you imagine tasting with your hands?

Watch this: in about one second, it can change from smooth and reddish-brown to bumpy and gray — like turning into a rock. Count "one!" as fast as you can. That's how fast it changes.

{% figure "/assets/blog/world-octopus-day-2026/scale.jpg", "Illustrated scale diagram: a giant Pacific octopus with its arms stretched out, measuring 4 metres (13 feet) from arm tip to arm tip, next to a standing five-year-old for scale", "Arms stretched all the way out, a big grown-up octopus measures about three and a half five-year-olds from arm tip to arm tip." %}

That's a lot of octopus. But it starts out tiny.

{% figure "/assets/blog/world-octopus-day-2026/hatchling.jpg", "A tiny, see-through baby giant Pacific octopus with orange speckles, drifting in dark ocean water", "A newly hatched giant Pacific octopus is about the size of a grain of rice." %}

Can you find a grain of rice in your kitchen? That's how small it starts.

First it drifts in the open ocean like a tiny speck. Then it settles down on the seafloor and grows up in a den, tucked into a crack between rocks.

{% figure "/assets/blog/world-octopus-day-2026/habitat.jpg", "A giant Pacific octopus den in a rocky crevice, with a pile of empty shells outside the entrance", "See that little pile of shells outside the den? Scientists call it a midden. We like to call it the octopus's \"front porch.\"" %}

Look outside its den — those are leftover shells from crabs and clams it's eaten, stacked up like a front porch.

Three hearts. Blue blood. Nine "brains." One amazing octopus.
