  grownups: |
    "Poison dart frog" covers a whole family of species, so there isn't one conservation status. The golden poison frog is listed as Endangered by the IUCN, and many other species are threatened or too little-studied to rate. The main worries are rainforest loss, the illegal pet trade, and a fungus disease that harms frogs.

    There's a hopeful story: in July 2025, Panama's Amphibian Rescue and Conservation Project moved 12 pairs of wild Pratt's poison frogs, after a health check and treatment against the fungus, into Altos de Campana National Park, where the species had been missing for at least 19 years. Researchers hope the frogs will breed on their own, and one pair already has. It's an early step, and the hope is that these frogs help the species return and stay strong.

    One family hope action: next time you shop, look for a "sustainable" label together, such as on paper or wood products. Protecting forests starts with everyday choices.

Some frogs wear colors so bright, they look like jewels on the forest floor!

{% figure "/assets/animals/poison_dart_frog/cover.jpg", "A bright blue poison dart frog with black spots standing on a mossy log in a sunlit rainforest, with a tiny green beetle nearby" %}

Meet the poison dart frog. It lives in the rainforests of Central and South America. It can be blue, red, yellow, green, or spotty like a strawberry!

Those bright colors are a message. They say, "Stay away — I don't taste good!" In the wild, the frog gets its poison from the tiny bugs it eats, like ants and mites. In a zoo, the frogs eat different bugs, so they aren't poisonous at all.

Most frogs sleep all day and hop around at night. Not the poison dart frog. It's out and about in the daytime, chirping and trilling to its friends.

Can you make a little chirpy frog sound?

{% figure "/assets/animals/poison_dart_frog/scale.jpg", "A girl explorer holding a jar, with a tiny poison dart frog shown beside her at its real size and a zoomed-in picture of a blue and yellow frog in a box" %}

Poison dart frogs are tiny. A big one, like the blue one in our picture, is only about 4 centimeters (1.5 inches) long. That's shorter than your finger!

{% figure "/assets/animals/poison_dart_frog/weight.jpg", "A scale showing about 6,000 poison dart frogs, as tiny dots, weighing the same as one 5-year-old child" %}

A poison dart frog weighs about 3 grams (0.1 ounce). You would need about 6,000 of them on one big scale to weigh as much as one 5-year-old!

Can you picture a whole crowd of tiny frogs, hopping onto a scale?

And here's the sweetest part. Many poison dart frog parents take great care of their babies. In some kinds, dad frogs carry the tadpoles on their backs. They give them a piggyback ride up into the trees, to a tiny pool of water, tucked in a leaf.

{% figure "/assets/animals/poison_dart_frog/baby.jpg", "A tiny dark green poison dart frog tadpole with legs and a long tail, standing on a leaf under shallow, clear water" %}

In some kinds, mom frogs even come back to feed their tadpoles special eggs until they grow legs.

Can you tuck yourself in tight, like a tadpole in a leaf?
