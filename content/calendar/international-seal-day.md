    Harp seals are listed as **Near Threatened** by the IUCN (2025), up from Least Concern, mainly because the sea ice they need for giving birth, nursing, and resting is melting as the Arctic warms about four times faster than the rest of the world. There are still millions of harp seals — one of the most numerous seals on Earth — and there's a hopeful precedent: by the late 1960s, heavy hunting had cut the Northwest Atlantic herd to about 2 million, but in 1987 Canada banned the commercial hunting of whitecoat pups, and the herd grew back into the millions. Hunting of older seals continues legally today, so the story isn't finished, but it shows that when people change the rules, seal numbers can bounce back. And in March 2026, a young harp seal found weak and dehydrated on a beach in Barnstable, Massachusetts, was nursed back to health at Mystic Aquarium and released to the ocean on March 20, 2026 — just two days before International Day of the Seal.

A baby harp seal is born on floating ice in a fluffy white coat. In less than two weeks, drinking its mom's super-rich milk, it grows to three times its birth weight!

{% figure "/assets/blog/international-seal-day/cover.jpg", "Harp seals swim under Arctic sea ice — Arctic sea ice and cold North Atlantic waters" %}

A brand-new harp seal pup is already about as heavy as a big bag of dog food, and almost as long as you are tall. Harp seal milk is super creamy — much, much richer than the milk in your fridge — and the pup gains weight fast. By the time it's ready to be on its own, it weighs as much as two five-year-olds.

Can you guess how a grown-up harp seal got its name? Look closely at its back — there's a dark, curvy mark shaped just like a harp!

{% figure "/assets/blog/international-seal-day/scale.jpg", "A grown-up harp seal lying beside a 5-year-old for scale" %}

A grown-up harp seal is about as long as one and a half kids lying head to toe in a row, and it weighs about as much as seven or eight five-year-olds put together.

{% figure "/assets/blog/international-seal-day/weight.jpg", "A harp seal on one scale (130 kg, 287 lb) and eight 5-year-olds on the other (148 kg, 326 lb)" %}

It takes eight kids on the other side of the scale to out-weigh one seal — can you count them?

Harp seals can hold their breath for about as long as a whole cartoon episode, and dive about 400 meters (1,300 feet) down — deeper than most skyscrapers are tall. Underwater, they're chatty too — they warble, trill, and chirp, and pups call out to their moms.

{% figure "/assets/blog/international-seal-day/baby.jpg", "A fluffy white harp seal pup lying on snowy sea ice" %}

Baby harp seals wait on the ice until they're big and strong enough to swim. While they wait, the fluffy white coat falls away, and a smooth grey coat is underneath.

Can you curl up small and fluffy like a baby seal on the ice? Can you wiggle across the floor like a seal, without using your hands?

Harp seals live in the icy North Atlantic and Arctic Ocean, near Canada, Greenland, Norway, and Russia — and every March, more fluffy white pups are born on the sea ice. Seal pups often rest alone on the beach while their mom is off fishing — that's normal! If you ever see one, the kind thing to do is watch quietly from far away and tell a grown-up.
