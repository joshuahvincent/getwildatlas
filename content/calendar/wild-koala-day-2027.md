    Koalas are listed as Vulnerable, which means there aren't as many of them as there used to be in many places. The biggest reason is habitat loss, as the eucalyptus forests they depend on are cleared for towns, farms and roads — along with bushfires, cars, and a disease called chlamydia.

    There's a hopeful story: in September 2025, after more than ten years of research, scientists in Australia won approval for the world's first chlamydia vaccine for koalas. In one wild population that's now being vaccinated, infections have dropped by about 75%, and dozens of healthy joeys have been born since.

A brand-new koala baby is about the size of a jellybean — and it climbs into its mom's pouch all by itself!

{% figure "/assets/blog/wild-koala-day-2027/cover.jpg", "A koala climbing along a eucalyptus branch in warm late-afternoon light" %}

A koala isn't a bear at all. It's a marsupial — a pouch animal, just like a kangaroo — and its closest living relatives are actually wombats. Koalas live high in the eucalyptus, or gum, trees of eastern Australia, and nowhere else in the world.

Koalas are picky eaters. They munch almost nothing but eucalyptus leaves, and each koala has a few favorite kinds out of hundreds. Those leaves don't give much energy, so a koala's tummy works slowly to break them down — which is why koalas rest or sleep for up to 18 to 20 hours a day!

{% figure "/assets/blog/wild-koala-day-2027/scale.jpg", "A koala beside the Wild Atlas child explorer for scale" %}

A grown-up koala is much shorter than you — about 75 centimeters (2 feet 6 inches) long, roughly up to your hip if you stood side by side.

{% figure "/assets/blog/wild-koala-day-2027/weight.jpg", "A scale with two koalas compared to a scale with one 5-year-old child" %}

Picture two grown-up koalas together on one big scale. Together, they'd weigh about as much as one 5-year-old — about 20 kilograms (44 pounds).

{% figure "/assets/blog/wild-koala-day-2027/baby.jpg", "A young koala gripping a eucalyptus trunk" %}

When a koala joey is born, it's about 2 centimeters long — smaller than your thumb — and weighs less than a gram. It can't see or hear yet, but it knows exactly where to go: straight into its mom's pouch, where it grows for months before it's ready to ride on her back.

Koalas hardly ever need a drink of water — their leaves are so full of water that they barely have to. And when a koala wants to be heard, it can let out a huge, rumbly bellow, using a special extra part in its throat that lets a small animal sound enormous. Here's one more strange thing: koalas have fingerprints that look a lot like yours!

Can you curl up as small as you can and pretend to snooze in a tree for a whole minute, just like a koala? Can you make the deepest, rumbliest sound you can — as deep as a koala's bellow? Can you tiptoe like you're trying not to wake a sleeping koala?
